# Master Reflection — My Whole Time at HCC

When I started at HCC in the summer of 2024, I didn't really know what I was walking into. My first classes were basic Python and a history of AI course. The Python was short exercises — chapters, sets, small programs that ran or didn't. The history course was a team project. I wasn't thinking about a career yet. I was just getting started.

The first thing that actually grabbed me was machine learning. In the fall of 2024 I took machine learning, computer vision, and natural language processing all at once, and for the first time the work felt real. I built a linear regression model. I trained a classifier on CIFAR-10 images. I worked through NLP labs with the NLTK toolkit. It was the first time I could point at something and say, I built that, and it works.

After that I went wider instead of deeper. Cybersecurity taught me the hands-on side — TryHackMe labs, real tools, and how much of security is just paying attention. Data science and database theory were the quiet courses, the ones nobody brags about, but they mattered: if your data is a mess, nothing else works. That came back later in data curation, where I built a scraper that collected real job listings and a script that cleaned HTML. It also came back in the SeniorSite project plan, which started as a class project and turned into a real system I put on GitHub.

Deep learning was the hardest stretch. CNNs, diffusion models, a final project that took real work. I learned that the models only do what the data lets them do, and that the boring parts — cleaning data, checking results, reading the numbers honestly — are most of the job.

Spring 2026 pulled everything toward things I actually care about. AI applications across industries. AI in business and finance, where the Scout AI trade advisor started. AI in robotics, where my team built a robot for the HumansNotFound project. Robot operations, where we worked with neural networks on real hardware ideas. That's when it stopped feeling like school and started feeling like a direction.

Fall 2026 is where it all came together. In 5G I simulated network traffic and trained a model to predict latency and pick routes. In digital twins I built a 3D-printed wall panel in Blender and exported it as OpenUSD — a real piece of a real building, modeled as a twin. In AI policy I wrote about what happens when these systems make decisions about people, and what rights people should have when they do. The technical work and the responsibility work belong together. I know that now.

What I learned about myself is simple: I learn by building. Reading about a model doesn't teach me anything until I've trained one and watched it fail. And I learned that explaining something in plain language is part of understanding it — if I can't say what a model does without jargon, I don't really understand it yet.

Where I'm headed is the thing that pulls all of this together. I want to work in 3D-printed construction technology — printing real homes and structures — and bring real estate and AI into the same business. The digital twin work, the site selection system, the modeling, the data work: they all point the same way. That's the direction I'm building toward, and this portfolio is the record of how I got here.
