How it works — Before

What the applicant sees first, and why each piece is there. The numbered markers match the screen.

    How it works, start to finish

    One rule runs through all three stages, straight from the paper: nothing important about the decision should be hidden behind a second click that nobody finds, or written in language nobody understands.

    Stage 1 · Before

    Notice first, submit second

    The applicant learns an AI system is involved, what data it will use, and that a human will review any denial on request — before submitting anything. The explanation page is one paragraph of plain language, no jargon.

    Stage 2 · During

    Data in the open, corrections live

    Every category of data in use is listed while the review runs — including the surprising one. A wrong fact can be corrected on the spot, which is cheaper and fairer than appealing a denial built on bad data.

    Stage 3 · After

    Reasons you can act on

    The result arrives with its principal reasons, the fact behind each one, and one honest change that would alter the outcome — plus a human-review button that opens a real case and a downloadable copy of the record.

    Design rules behind the screen

    The rules from the end of Part 2 that hold the whole design together — each one is visible somewhere in the mockup above.

    ✎

    Plain language, tested with real users

    Every explanation is written at a reading level an ordinary applicant can follow, and tested with real users before launch — not just with engineers.

    #

    Numbers are explained, not just shown

    The score appears with what it means and what range counts as passing (612 vs. a 640 threshold, on a 300–850 scale) — because a bare number is not an explanation.

    ◐

    Accessibility is part of transparency

    The panel works with screen readers and large text, and never lets color alone carry the meaning — the denial is stated in words, not just shown in red. A notice some users cannot read is not a notice at all.

    ⚖

    Grounded in the NIST AI RMF

    A trustworthy system has to be accountable and transparent, and explainable enough that a person can understand why it did what it did (National Institute of Standards and Technology).

    What this mockup does — and does not — prove

    In the paper’s own words: the interface does not solve every problem in Part 1. A lender still has to build a model that can produce real reasons, and a human reviewer still has to have time and authority to overturn a result. What the interface does is make the right to explanation usable. Screenshot any stage above — the caption under the mockup is written to drop straight into the paper as a figure caption.

    From Part 2 of the paper “A right that lives in a policy document, but never appears on the screen where the decision lands, is not a right the user actually has.”
